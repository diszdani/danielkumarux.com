Product context

Archon Data Store (ADS) is an enterprise data management and storage platform built by Platform 3 Solutions. It works alongside Archon ETL: ETL extracts, transforms, and loads data from legacy applications, while ADS manages the resulting archived data through application and metadata management, search and retrieval, compliance and retention, data governance, ingestion monitoring, and administration.

Business problem and product origin

Large enterprises often retain years of historical data in live production systems, adding ongoing storage costs. Archiving moves older data out of production while keeping it available for future business needs. Platform 3 Solutions previously relied on a third-party data management and storage platform alongside Archon ETL. The CEO identified an opportunity to build an in-house alternative, which led to ADS.

My role

I joined Platform 3 Solutions as Senior UX Lead in February 2019 and began leading ADS UX in 2022, around its first documented release. I continued through September 2026. My responsibilities included UX strategy, information architecture, user flows, wireframes, high-fidelity UI design, design system development, Figma and Dev Mode handoffs, implementation reviews, and UX issue tracking through GitHub. Junior designers worked under my direct supervision on individual screens, while I retained overall responsibility for UX direction, quality, and consistency.

Section 2

Users, Roles and the Design Challenge

ADS serves five distinct user roles across a wide range of technical expertise. Designing for all of them within a single product, without creating separate tools, was the central structural challenge.

The five roles

Role Access and expertise Primary responsibilities
Administrator Broadest product access Full access across the product: creates applications, manages searches and metadata, configures compliance policies, controls users, groups, permissions, storage, and system-level jobs
Developer High technical expertise Creates and configures applications, sets up metadata schemas, authors search screens, writes and updates queries to make the product usable for other roles
Retention Manager Deep compliance expertise Creates and manages retention, hold, and compliance policies; reviews and approves disposal lists; applies compliance at application, table, and record level
Auditor Read-only access Reviews system activity, audit reports, and chain of custody records for organisational auditing or legal purposes
End User Low technical expertise Runs pre-built searches to view and export data; typically business, legal, or support staff

The design tension

The Developer and the End User represent opposite ends of the expertise range, yet both work within the same product. The Developer configures the search screens the End User runs. The Retention Manager approves disposals that the Developer’s application configuration governs. These overlapping but distinct responsibilities meant the product had to serve radically different users without compromising either.

Navigation and information architecture

The navigation model organises the product across three audience planes, separating content by who needs it:

  • Application workspace: where Developers, End Users, and Retention Managers work within a specific application, accessing Searches, Metadata, Compliance settings, and App Config
  • System Management: where Administrators manage users, groups, permissions, storage, and system-level configuration
  • Data Governance: where Retention Managers, Auditors, and Administrators work with compliance policy, data classification, audit records, and lineage across the product

Within the application workspace, navigation is scoped to the selected application. The main navigation also provides access to product-wide areas such as Data Governance, System Management, Cross App Search, Status Monitoring, and Background Jobs. Entering an application surfaces its application-specific features, with a back link to the application list. This kept system configuration tools out of the workspace of users who had no need for them.

One hierarchy, expressed consistently

The product organises data at four levels: application, schema, table, and column. Maintaining a consistent hierarchy and interaction pattern across functional areas was intended to make it easier for users to understand and navigate the product.

Authoring and consumption

Search illustrates the expertise gap most directly. Developers author search screens, defining parameters, result columns, permissions, and retrieval logic. End Users run those pre-built screens to view and export archived records. The two surfaces are distinct: the authoring surface exposes configuration options the End User never sees; the audience surface presents a clean interface scoped to what that user is permitted to do. The same search definition drives both.

Section 3

Key Design Decisions

Four decisions that shaped the product’s direction more than any others.

Dashboard refresh strategy

The ADS application dashboard aggregates data across several areas: storage metrics, catalog scores, compliance status, job activity, ingestion trends, and search and export summaries. My initial proposal was to provide real-time updates across all dashboard widgets so users would always see current information.

During technical discussions with Engineering, I learned that continuously fetching data across multiple clusters would increase API load and risk overall system performance. Rather than abandoning data freshness entirely, I worked with Engineering to understand which metrics required more frequent updates and which could tolerate longer intervals.

The result was an approach combining configurable refresh intervals, a manual refresh option, and clear last-updated timestamps on relevant sections. This kept the dashboard useful without creating a system performance trade-off, and made the data currency visible to users rather than hiding the limitation.

Separating Run from Export

Search results were originally controlled through a single split button that combined run and export actions. A client requirement introduced a new constraint: some user groups needed to view search results but not be permitted to export them.

The combined control made it impossible to enforce that permission at the required level of granularity. I separated the interface into a Run menu and a distinct Export menu, each governed by its own permission. Engineering extended the permission model to support the split. The change allowed access control to be applied precisely, without the UI obscuring the distinction between viewing and exporting.

Search: four types, one interface

ADS search users range from Developers writing complex SQL queries to End Users running pre-built searches with no technical knowledge. The challenge was serving both within the same tool, without forcing expert users to context-switch to another product or overwhelming novice users with options they did not need.

The solution was a set of four search types within one interface:

  • Basic: query auto-generated from selected fields
  • Advanced: hand-written SQL for complex retrieval
  • Report: formatted output with layout control
  • Group: virtual folder combining multiple searches

The four types serve different search and output needs within the same interface. Users choose the type that fits the task without switching to another tool. Three result layout options were also evaluated during design: a card layout and a dense grid were explored and rejected because both repeated labels inefficiently and the grid offered no row selection for export or rehydration actions. A data table with row selection was shipped: labels appear once, values align in columns, and row selection supports both export and rehydration workflows.

Structural safeguards over confirmation dialogs

In a compliance-governed product where many operations cannot be undone, risk design cannot rely on users reading confirmation dialogs carefully. The design approach was to build safeguards into the architecture so that irreversible actions are separated, sequenced, or blocked, not merely warned against.

Specific decisions:

  • Disposal list generation is separated from approval, and approval is assigned to the Retention Manager role. The two steps cannot be performed by the same action.
  • User deletion is blocked until cross-application search ownership is transferred. The deletion dialog states irreversibility explicitly, separates permanently deleted items from retained ones, and keeps the delete control disabled until the transfer and acknowledgement are both complete.
  • Protected storage configuration locks once a table holds records. This constraint is stated at the point of configuration, not discovered later.
  • Metadata columns can be added but never removed. The additive-only constraint is shown at the point of addition.
  • Application deletion is blocked while data remains, with the reason displayed.

Section 4

Key Workflows

Data rehydration

Rehydration writes archived data back into operational databases, a high-stakes operation where errors can affect live systems. The design catches problems before execution rather than after.

The workflow runs in stages:

  1. Data lands in a staging environment first. The production push is a separate, explicitly approved step.
  2. A field-by-field check identifies missing metadata before the data moves. Users can create missing metadata directly from this step.
  3. Related tables are presented for inclusion. Excluding them requires the user to acknowledge the risk of orphaned records.
  4. The approver reviews an impact summary before confirming. Rejection requires written remarks.
  5. Scheduling accepts a start time only. An end window was not technically feasible; the constraint is surfaced in the UI rather than hidden.

This sequence is designed to surface data integrity risks before they reach production, while making the consequences of each stage visible before the user commits to it.

Multi-stage approvals

Approval workflows in ADS can involve individuals or groups across multiple sequential stages, covering data export approvals, compliance reviews, and other governance actions.

The design includes:

  • A stage builder with a running count of approvers, so the structure of an approval chain is visible as it is configured
  • Separate inboxes for requests received and requests raised, keeping the two contexts distinct
  • A review screen that marks the current stage as the active one for the reviewer
  • The ability to send a request back to an earlier stage, with remarks required

The workflow design had to make governance feel like a managed process rather than a bottleneck. Separating inboxes, surfacing stage position, and enabling rollback within the chain all serve that goal.

Data quality and the catalog

The catalog scores description quality for applications, schemas, tables, and columns. Each score rolls up through the hierarchy and carries a recommendation for improvement.

During implementation review, a QA finding identified that search results showed a matching parent object but hid its child objects; users interpreted empty child results as missing data rather than unexpanded matches. The fix was written as user stories: matches expand to include their children, with matching children listed first. This was caught through the implementation review process rather than post-release.

Section 5

Design System and Process

Design system

The design system covered colour, typography, spacing, elevation, controls, and data table states, including header, row, empty, loading, and filter states. Engineering used token names rather than hex values, which kept the light and dark themes in step without requiring separate component sets.

Standardised shells across the product included the creation wizard with early exit, the job scheduling pattern, the review workflow, the people picker, and hierarchical drill-down. Building these as reusable patterns meant that new modules could be designed and built consistently, without requiring UX involvement in every individual build decision. The design system grew to 27 pages over the course of the engagement.

One lesson from the engagement: the modern UI guidelines for ADS arrived after some modules had already shipped. Foundational primitives (spacing, control heights, states, colour roles) do not depend on product maturity and should be established early. Composite patterns such as wizards, scheduling shells, and review workflows reasonably waited until they had recurred enough to be worth standardising.

Design and collaboration process

Each feature followed a consistent path:

  1. Requirements review with Business Analysts and Product stakeholders
  2. User flow definition and workflow mapping
  3. Wireframes and proposed solution discussion
  4. Stakeholder and Engineering review for technical feasibility
  5. High-fidelity design, with junior designer support on individual screens where appropriate
  6. Figma and Dev Mode handoff
  7. Implemented screen review
  8. UX issue tracking through GitHub
  9. Stakeholder reviews and product demonstrations

A design sign-off gate was written into requirements before high-fidelity work began. This meant rough ideation and UX review happened before build commitment, surfacing structural issues earlier in the process.

One decision that changed through this process: data rehydration scheduling was initially designed as a side panel. During technical review, it became clear that the filter area and table references required by the scheduling flow would not fit comfortably in a panel. The design moved to a dedicated page, which also gave the step more appropriate visual weight given its consequences.

Section 6

Outcomes and Reflection

What shipped

Over the course of the engagement, the following features were designed and delivered: the application dashboard, approval workflow, data rehydration, protected storage and tokenisation, bulk CSV search, compliance and retention management, and the data governance module.

Observed outcomes

No quantified metrics were captured during the engagement, so none are claimed here.

The design aimed to make the product usable across a wide expertise range without requiring technical knowledge for every task. The search authoring and consumption model, the structured rehydration workflow, and the role-based access controls each addressed specific points where the wrong design would have required End Users or Retention Managers to depend on a Developer to complete their work.

The design system gave Engineering a consistent reference as new modules were added across multiple release cycles.

Reflection

Four years on a product this complex surfaces patterns that are only visible in retrospect. Foundational design primitives (spacing scales, control heights, state definitions, colour roles) should be settled early, before modules ship. They are independent of product decisions and can be established without waiting for the product to mature.

The design system itself was as much a negotiation as a design exercise. Aligning on shared rules across a product of this scope, with engineering teams working in parallel, required as much stakeholder management as it did component design.

“The interface was never the hard part. Agreeing on one set of rules was.”
